Turning 20 ideas into a monthly posting calendar

A list of ideas only helps if it turns into a habit. Loan officers who keep a Google Business Profile active tend to batch a month of posts in one sitting rather than staring at a blank box every Monday. Here is a practical way to spread the 20 ideas above across four weeks, grouped by what they are actually doing for you — visibility, trust, or conversion.

Week 1: Market context, kept compliant

Lead with something timely that does not cross into advertised terms. Instead of quoting a specific rate or promising a payment, write about the direction of the market and what it means for someone's decision timeline — for example, "Buyers are asking us whether to wait or move now — here's how we help them decide." Pair it with a link to a local market page so the post has somewhere real to send traffic.

Week 2: Local and seasonal tie-ins

Tie a post to something happening in your actual market — a school district's open-enrollment window, a local home tour, a holiday moving season, a first-time buyer workshop at the library. These posts perform well because they read as local news, not sales copy, and they signal to Google that the profile is tied to a real place, not a template.

Week 3: First-time buyer and product education

Break down one concept per post: down payment assistance, what a pre-approval letter actually protects, the difference between a rate and an APR, or how a DSCR loan works for a self-employed borrower. Keep each post to a single idea — profiles that try to explain three products in one post lose readers before the third sentence.

Week 4: Behind-the-scenes and team posts

Introduce yourself, your process, or a team member. A short "why I do this work" post or a photo from a closing (with the client's permission) humanizes a profile that would otherwise read as a directory listing. These posts are also the easiest to write — they do not require market research, just a few honest sentences.

Writing GBP post copy that stays compliant

Google Business Profile posts are marketing communications, which means the same guardrails that apply to any other advertising apply here. A few practical habits keep posts useful without creating compliance exposure:

  • Avoid quoting a specific rate or payment in the post itself — rates move daily and a screenshot of an old post with an old number can outlive its accuracy by months.
  • Skip guarantee language like "guaranteed approval" or "lowest rate in [city]" — these are the phrases that trigger both regulatory and platform scrutiny.
  • Include your NMLS number in your profile and keep it visible, even if it does not fit naturally into every individual post.
  • Get permission before posting client details, even a first name or neighborhood, and keep the language general enough that no individual borrower's file is identifiable.
  • Route the call-to-action to a page you control — a borrower page under your own name rather than a generic "call now" with no context keeps the post accountable to what it actually delivers.

FAQ: posting cadence and compliance

How often should I actually post to Google Business Profile?

Once a week is a realistic floor that keeps the profile signaling activity to Google without becoming a chore you abandon after a month. If you can sustain twice a week without repeating yourself, that is a reasonable ceiling — posting daily rarely adds value and usually means the content quality drops to hit the schedule.

Do the same advertising compliance rules apply to GBP posts as to paid ads?

Treat them the same way. GBP posts are public-facing marketing content, so avoid quoted rates, guaranteed-outcome language, and anything that could be read as a commitment to lend. When in doubt, run new post formats past your compliance process the same way you would a flyer or a paid ad.

Can I reuse the same post idea across different months?

Yes, with fresh specifics. A "down payment assistance" post in March and again in September is fine as long as the details, examples, or local programs referenced are current — what you should avoid is republishing the identical wording, since that reads as filler to both readers and to Google.
