Reviews are one of the top three local ranking factors — and one of the biggest trust signals a borrower sees. More (genuine) reviews on your Google Business Profile means higher rankings and more calls. Here is a system that actually gets them.

Ask at the moment of maximum happiness

The best time to ask is right after closing, when the client is thrilled. Waiting a week kills your response rate.

Make it one tap

Send a direct review link (Google gives you a short one in your profile dashboard) by text. The fewer steps, the more reviews. "If you have 30 seconds, it would mean a lot: [link]."

Ask everyone, every time

Build it into your closing process so it is automatic, not something you remember sometimes. Consistency is what compounds.

Tell them what helps

A quick nudge — "mentioning your loan type and city really helps others find me" — produces richer reviews that also help your local rankings.

Respond to every review

Thank positive reviewers by name. Respond calmly and professionally to any negative one. Google rewards engagement, and prospects read your responses.

Never buy or fake reviews

Fake reviews violate Google's policies and can get your profile suspended — losing everything you built. Earn them honestly; it is the only durable way.

Make it a referral loop

Happy reviewers are happy referrers. The same closing-day moment that earns a review is the moment to ask, "Know anyone else buying or refinancing?"

A steady drip of genuine reviews quietly lifts your local rankings month after month — free, durable, and impossible for competitors to copy.

When to actually send the request: why "right after closing" beats every other timing

Timing is the single biggest lever in review generation, and mortgage has a timing quirk most other industries do not: the emotional peak is not the sale, it is the close. A borrower who just got keys, or just lowered their payment on a refinance, is relieved and grateful in a way that fades fast — often within 48 to 72 hours, once movers, utility transfers, and the next life task take over. Asking during the loan process is the wrong moment twice over: the outcome is not certain yet, so you risk associating the ask with stress rather than relief, and in some states solicitation during an open transaction can brush up against advertising and consumer-protection rules your compliance department will want you to avoid entirely. The request belongs after the deal has funded and disclosures have run their course — not before.

In practice that means building the ask into your closing-day or closing-week checklist as a fixed step, not a maybe. If you rely on memory, you will forget on your busiest weeks — which are usually your best closings. Automate the trigger off your closed-loan status in your CRM or pipeline tool so the request goes out on a set schedule (same day for a text, next business day for an email) instead of whenever you happen to think of it.

Ask scripts that convert without sounding scripted

The best-performing asks are short, specific to what just happened, and make the action a single tap. Generic "please review us" messages get ignored; a message that references the borrower's own experience gets opened and acted on. A few templates that hold up well under compliance review because they make no promises, offer nothing of value, and simply ask for an honest account of the experience:

  • Text, same day as closing: "Congrats again on your new home, [Name]! If you have 60 seconds, a quick honest review of your experience would mean a lot and helps other buyers find a loan officer they can trust: [Google review link]."
  • Email, 1–2 business days after closing: "It was a pleasure helping you get to the closing table. If you have a moment, I'd be grateful for a short review of how the process went for you — the good and the areas we can improve. Here's the link: [Google review link]."
  • In person, at the closing table (verbal only, follow up with the link): "I'll send you a quick link later today — if you're willing, an honest review helps other buyers find someone who'll walk them through this the way I walked you through it."

Notice what is absent from every script above: no gift card, no drawing, no discount, no "as a thank-you." That is intentional, and it is the most important compliance line in this entire article — see the FAQ below.

Automating the request without making it feel like a robot

Manual asks do not scale past a handful of closings a month, but automation done badly reads as impersonal and gets ignored or, worse, gets marked as spam. The fix is a short, personalized drip rather than a single blast: an initial text or email sent by an automation tool but written in your voice and referencing the specific transaction, followed by one polite reminder 5–7 days later only if no review has posted, then stop. Asking a third or fourth time damages the relationship for a marginal lift in review count. Responding to negative reviews without making it worse

Every loan officer who closes enough volume eventually gets an unfair or emotionally charged negative review — often from a deal that did not close, or from a borrower frustrated with a rate environment or underwriting condition outside your control. The instinct to defend yourself publicly, or to explain loan-specific details in the response, is the wrong move on two fronts: it rarely changes the reviewer's mind, and discussing another person's loan details publicly can raise privacy concerns. The professional pattern is short and consistent: acknowledge the frustration without conceding fault you do not owe, thank them for the feedback, and move the specifics offline. For example: "I'm sorry to hear the process didn't meet your expectations. I'd welcome the chance to discuss this directly — please call or email me at [contact] so I can understand what happened." Do not argue in the reply thread, do not disclose transaction details, and do not delete or hide legitimate negative reviews — a single calm, professional response to an outlier negative review often reads as a trust signal to everyone else who reads it.

FAQ: getting reviews the right way

Can I offer a gift card, discount, or entry into a drawing for leaving a review?

No — never offer anything of value in exchange for a review, incentivized or conditional review requests violate Google's review policies and can get your Business Profile's reviews removed or the profile suspended entirely, and tying compensation to a review can also raise separate advertising and consumer-protection concerns for licensed originators. Ask for honest feedback, full stop, with nothing attached.

How soon after closing should I actually send the request?

Same day to within 48 hours is the sweet spot. The gratitude that follows a closing fades quickly once the borrower moves on to unpacking boxes or enjoying a lower payment, so a same-day text or a next-business-day email consistently outperforms anything sent a week or more later.

Is it ever okay to ask for a review before the loan closes?

No. Asking mid-transaction risks tying the request to an uncertain outcome and can look like solicitation during an open file. Wait until the loan has funded and disclosures are complete before asking.

How should I respond to a negative review from someone whose loan didn't close?

Keep it short, professional, and off the specifics: acknowledge their frustration, avoid discussing loan details publicly, and invite them to contact you directly to resolve it. Never argue in the reply thread, and never delete a legitimate negative review — a calm response is itself a trust signal to future readers.
